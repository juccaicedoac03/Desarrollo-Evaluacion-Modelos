Warm-up · 10'

Warm-up · Q1

You only have 30 labelled examples. Which classifier usually gets closer to its best possible error first?

Ng & Jordan (2002): generative classifiers such as Naive Bayes approach their (higher) asymptotic error with fewer examples; logistic regression usually wins once data is plentiful. That is why the learning curve in the S08 lab crossed over.
[0:02 · 3 min] If the room hesitates, sketch the two learning curves: Naive Bayes drops fast and plateaus higher; logistic regression starts worse and ends lower. Business link for today: with little data, the "simple" option can be the right first prototype.
Warm-up · Q2

In zero-shot classification by label likelihood, what does the LLM compute for each label?

We score \( \log p(\text{label tokens} \mid \text{prompt}) \) for every verbalized label and pick the arg max. No training, but the choice of verbalizer (“bullish” vs “positive”) matters. We use it again today in the finance part of the lab.
[0:05 · 2 min] Connect to today: in the lab we compare this zero-shot LLM against FinBERT, a model fine-tuned on financial text, on the same dataset as S08.
Warm-up · Q3

A teammate fitted TF-IDF on the whole dataset and tuned the decision threshold on the test set. The report says 94% accuracy. What do you answer?

Both are data leakage. Unsupervised statistics (vocabulary, IDF) computed on test data and a threshold tuned on test data both make the reported number look better than what production will see. Today this matters twice: a business case built on a leaky metric overstates the ROI.
[0:07 · 3 min] This bridges to today: every ROI estimate we build starts from a quality number. If that number is inflated by leakage, the whole business case is inflated. Transition: "Let's see how to turn a model metric into a decision."

[0:24 · 2 min] Each level answers to a different owner. A model can improve its accuracy and the business outcome stays flat (nobody uses it), or the operational KPI improves while a guardrail breaks (faster answers, more complaints). The canvas in the challenge and in H3 asks for at least one KPI and one guardrail. Without a baseline, you cannot compute any improvement.

ROI and payback

A transparent formula beats a big number

Hours saved per month, net of rework:

$$ H = \frac{V \cdot a \cdot \big(q\,m - (1-q)\,r\big)}{60} $$
$$ B = H \cdot c - C_{\text{run}} \qquad \text{payback} = \frac{C_{\text{impl}}}{B}\ \ (B > 0) $$ $$ \text{ROI}_{12} = \frac{12\,H\,c - (C_{\text{impl}} + 12\,C_{\text{run}})}{C_{\text{impl}} + 12\,C_{\text{run}}} $$

\(V\) cases per month · \(a\) adoption · \(q\) share usable without rework · \(m\) minutes saved per usable output · \(r\) rework minutes per unusable output · \(c\) loaded cost per hour · \(C_{\text{impl}}\) one-off cost · \(C_{\text{run}}\) monthly cost (compute, licences, monitoring, review).

[0:26 · 2 min] Walk through each symbol. The quality q comes from YOUR measurement (the lab and challenge produce it). The rework term is the error cost from two slides ago: with q = 0.8 and rework minutes twice the minutes saved, 20% of cases eat half the savings of the other 80%. Same structure as block B5 of the project template. The challenge's ROI cell implements exactly this formula.

Defaults: 10,000 cases, 60% adoption, 85% usable, 5 min saved, 8 min rework, USD 20/h, USD 60k one-off, USD 2.5k/month → net ≈ USD 3,600/month, payback ≈ 17 months, negative 12-month ROI. Check · payback

Hours saved are worth USD 8,000 per month, run costs are USD 3,000 per month and the one-off cost is USD 40,000. What is the payback?

Net monthly benefit = 8,000 − 3,000 = 5,000; payback = 40,000 / 5,000 = 8 months. Answer (a) forgets the run cost, the most common mistake in business cases.

Top-down estimate

The economic potential, and how to read it

USD 2.6–4.4 T

added value per year across the 63 use cases analysed by McKinsey (2023); about 75% of it in customer operations, marketing and sales, software engineering and R&D.

How to read it: a scenario estimate of potential at scale, not measured results. It says where to look, not what your pilot will return.

Use top-down numbers to pick where to search; use your own baseline and pilot to build the business case.

[0:32 · 2 min] Source: McKinsey & Company (June 2023), "The economic potential of generative AI: The next productivity frontier". Students saw the headline in S01; today the point is how to use it. Field evidence · customer support

Generative AI at work: support agents

+14%

issues resolved per hour, on average, after 5,179 support agents got an AI assistant (NBER working paper, 2023). The 2025 journal version reports about +15%.

  • Biggest gains for novices and lower-skilled agents (+34% in the working paper); little effect for the most experienced.
  • The assistant spreads the practices of top performers to everyone else.
  • Customers were more positive and asked less often for a manager.

Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2023, NBER w31161; QJE, 2025).

[0:34 · 3 min] One of the first large field studies with real workers and staggered rollout, so the comparison is credible. Key teaching points: (1) the effect is heterogeneous: onboarding and novice support are strong use cases; (2) the unit is a task inside a job, not the whole job; (3) the outcome is an operational KPI (resolutions per hour) plus guardrails (customer sentiment, escalations). Note the numbers differ slightly between the 2023 working paper (5,179 agents, +14%) and the 2025 QJE article (5,172 agents, +15%): always say which version you cite.

Field evidence · knowledge work

The jagged technological frontier

Inside the frontier

758 BCG consultants with GPT-4 completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with more than 40% higher quality ratings.

Outside the frontier

On a task designed to fall outside the model's capabilities, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer.

Takeaway: tasks that look equally hard can sit on opposite sides of the frontier. Map it with your own tests before you scale. “Centaurs” split tasks between human and AI; “cyborgs” interleave them.

Dell'Acqua, McFowland, Mollick, Lifshitz-Assaf, Kellogg, Rajendran, Krayer, Candelon & Lakhani (2023), HBS Working Paper 24-013.

[0:37 · 3 min] Figures from the 2023 HBS working paper (now published in Organization Science). The outside-the-frontier task looked like a normal business case but required spotting a subtle inconsistency in interviews; AI made people confidently wrong. Link to value × feasibility: "technical feasibility" means "is this task inside the frontier for this model?", and only a test set from your own task answers it.

From their study to your case

Does the evidence transfer?

Four transfer questions

  1. Same task? Support chats ≠ legal opinions.
  2. Same users? Novices gain more than experts.
  3. Same quality measure? Speed alone can hide errors.
  4. Same tool and context? Language, data, integration.

Budget a pilot with a comparison group and a baseline. It is the cheapest way to replace borrowed numbers with your own.

Sector · health

Health: high value, high stakes

Use cases

  • Clinical documentation drafts
  • Replies to patient messages
  • Intake and routing of requests
  • Coding and admin work

Evidence

Med-PaLM work (Singhal et al., 2023, Nature): 67.6% on USMLE-style MedQA with Flan-PaLM, yet clinician review still found gaps in the answers. Benchmark scores are not safety.

Risks

  • Under-triage of urgent cases
  • Automation bias in staff
  • Health data are sensitive data (Ley 1581 de 2012)
Lab framing: our symptom-retrieval prototype routes requests; it is not medical advice and a clinician always decides.
[0:43 · 3 min] Singhal et al. (2023) introduced MultiMedQA and HealthSearchQA; Flan-PaLM reached 67.6% on MedQA and instruction-tuned Med-PaLM improved clinician ratings, but the authors stress that the models were not ready for clinical use. Med-PaLM 2 (Singhal et al., 2025, Nature Medicine) improved further. The use cases with the most traction are administrative (documentation, messages), where a professional reviews every output. In Colombia, health data are sensitive data under Ley 1581: explicit authorization and reinforced security.

Sector · finance

Finance: documents, service and signals

Use cases

  • Customer-service assistants
  • Document processing (KYC, invoices, contracts)
  • Knowledge assistants for advisors
  • News and sentiment triage

Examples

Morgan Stanley launched a GPT-4-based assistant for its financial advisors to query internal research (2023). FinBERT (Araci, 2019) adapted BERT to financial sentiment.

Risks

  • Advice rules and supervision
  • Explainability of decisions
  • Fraud: deepfakes and phishing also use GenAI

In fraud, generative AI cuts both ways: it helps analysts summarise alerts, and it helps criminals produce convincing fakes. Budget for both sides.

[0:46 · 3 min] Business lens for Part 3. The advisor assistant is a textbook RAG use case: huge internal document base, expert users who can check answers, clear time savings. Customer-facing advice is harder: suitability rules and supervision. Today's lab compares FinBERT (fine-tuned, Araci 2019) with a zero-shot LLM on financial tweets.

Sector · education

Education: help that does not replace learning

Use cases

  • Tutoring: Khan Academy's Khanmigo guides with questions instead of giving answers
  • Feedback on drafts; question generation; teacher admin

Risks

  • Over-reliance, wrong content, unequal access, student data

Evidence · Bastani et al. (2025, PNAS)

Field experiment with about 1,000 high-school math students in Turkey:

  • Plain GPT-4 interface: practice grades +48%, but −17% on exams without AI
  • Tutor with guardrails: practice +127%, no significant harm on exams
[0:49 · 3 min] The key lesson: the design of the tool (guardrails, Socratic prompting) changes the outcome from harmful to helpful. Khanmigo is described qualitatively: a GPT-4-based tutor launched in 2023 that asks guiding questions rather than handing out answers. Today's lab generates quiz questions from science paragraphs and checks automatically whether they can be answered.

Documented failures

Your chatbot speaks for your organization

Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024 BCCRT 149)

The airline's website chatbot told a customer he could request a bereavement fare after travelling; the airline's policy said otherwise. Air Canada argued the chatbot was responsible for its own actions. The British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal rejected this, found negligent misrepresentation and ordered the airline to pay the fare difference plus interest and fees.

NYC MyCity chatbot (The Markup, 2024)

New York City's official AI chatbot for business owners gave answers that contradicted the law, for example that employers could take a cut of workers' tips and that landlords could refuse housing vouchers. The city kept the tool online with added disclaimers.

[1:00 · 3 min] Moffatt v. Air Canada, decided 14 February 2024 by the BC Civil Resolution Tribunal: damages of about CA$650 (the fare difference) plus pre-judgment interest and tribunal fees (about CA$812 in total). The tribunal said, in substance, that the company is responsible for all the information on its website, whether it comes from a static page or a chatbot. MyCity: The Markup (Colin Lecher, 29 March 2024) tested the Microsoft-powered chatbot launched by the city in October 2023. Both are about the same pattern: a fluent assistant answering outside its verified knowledge, with no human in the loop, in a domain (policy, law) where errors create liability.

Success factors

What successful deployments have in common

🎯 A costly, frequent task

Volume × minutes is large, and the output is checkable.

📏 Baseline and comparison

Measured before, with a control group or a staggered rollout.

📚 Knowledge with an owner

Documents are current, versioned and someone updates them.

🧑‍⚕️ Humans where harm is high

Autonomy grows only with measured quality.

🧪 Evaluation never stops

Test sets before launch, monitoring and audits after.

🤝 Adoption is designed

Training, workflow fit, clear accountability, kill criteria.

  • Field evidence is real but uneven: novices gain most, and some tasks fall outside the frontier.
  • Your assistant speaks for you: grounding, scope limits and humans where harm is high.
