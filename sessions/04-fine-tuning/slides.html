Part 1

How much of the model should change?

Full fine-tuning, parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and the maths of LoRA.

Theory · 60'
[0:10] Theory block: four parts. Part 1 (20 min) ends with the LoRA calculator and a quiz.

Fine-tuning in one line

Start from pretrained weights, keep training on your task

Pretraining gives weights \(\theta_0\). Fine-tuning minimises the loss on your labelled data, starting from \(\theta_0\) instead of random numbers:

$$ \theta^{*} = \arg\min_{\theta} \; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{L}\big(f_\theta(x_i),\, y_i\big), \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta_0 \text{ at the start} $$

1 · General LM

Pretrain a language model on a large general corpus.

2 · Target-domain LM

Continue language modelling on text from your domain.

3 · Task head

Fine-tune a classifier, unfreezing layers gradually, with layer-specific learning rates.

ULMFiT (Howard & Ruder, 2018) made this recipe standard in NLP: with 100 labelled examples it matched training from scratch on 100× more data.

[0:10 · 3 min] The formula is the same supervised objective as always; the only difference is the starting point. ULMFiT's three stages and its tricks (discriminative fine-tuning = different learning rates per layer; slanted triangular learning rates; gradual unfreezing) are the ancestors of today's warmup schedules and of the idea that lower layers should change less. The 100-example result is from the ULMFiT abstract. Stage 2 is what S03 called domain adaptation.

Two strategies

Full fine-tuning vs parameter-efficient fine-tuning

Full fine-tuningPEFT (adapters, LoRA, prompt tuning…)
What trainsEvery weight of the modelA small set of new weights; the original weights stay frozen
Training memoryWeights + gradients + optimizer state for all parametersFrozen weights + gradients and optimizer state for a tiny fraction
What you store per taskA full copy of the modelA small adapter file (often a few MB)
Forgetting riskHigher: all weights can driftLower, and the base can be recovered by removing the adapter
Typical useSmall encoders (DistilBERT, BERT) and plenty of computeLLMs, many tasks on one base model, limited GPUs

Today's lab does both: full fine-tuning of DistilBERT (67 M parameters) and LoRA on SmolLM2 (135 M).

[0:13 · 2 min] Go row by row. Lower forgetting risk with PEFT is a tendency, not a guarantee: a LoRA adapter can still change behaviour a lot (we will see it in the lab). The "base can be recovered" point is literal: in the lab we switch the adapter off with one line of code.

That gap is exactly what PEFT attacks: if only 0.1% of the parameters train, gradients and Adam states almost disappear.

The PEFT family

Four ways to train a few weights

🔌 Adapters

Small bottleneck layers inserted in every Transformer block; only they train. Within 0.4% of full fine-tuning on GLUE with 3.6% extra parameters per task.

Houlsby et al. (2019)

🧩 Prefix & prompt tuning

Learn a few continuous "virtual tokens" prepended to the input; the model is frozen. Close to full fine-tuning for very large models.

Li & Liang (2021) · Lester et al. (2021)

🪶 LoRA

Add a trainable low-rank update \(BA\) to chosen weight matrices. Can be merged after training: no extra inference latency.

Hu et al. (2021)

🗜️ QLoRA

LoRA on top of a base model quantized to 4 bits (NF4). Fine-tuned a 65 B model on a single 48 GB GPU.

Dettmers et al. (2023)

[0:17 · 3 min] One sentence per family; figures come from each paper's abstract. Adapters add layers (a small inference cost); prompt/prefix tuning adds tokens (uses context length); LoRA modifies existing matrices and can be merged away; QLoRA saves memory on the frozen base, not on the adapter. Hugging Face's PEFT library implements all of them with the same API; we use LoRA.

LoRA · the maths

Freeze \(W_0\), learn a low-rank update

$$ W = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + BA, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r},\; A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k},\; r \ll \min(d, k) $$ $$ h = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r}\, B A x $$
  • \(W_0\) (\(d \times k\)) is frozen; only \(A\) and \(B\) train.
  • \(A\) starts random, \(B\) starts at zero, so \(\Delta W = 0\) at step 0: training starts exactly from the pretrained model.
  • \(\alpha / r\) scales the update (in the lab: \(\alpha = 16,\ r = 8\) → ×2).

Parameters per matrix

Full update: \(d \cdot k\) · LoRA: \(r\,(d + k)\)

Example: \(d = k = 576\) (SmolLM2's query projection), \(r = 8\):

\(576^2 = 331{,}776\) vs \(8 \times 1{,}152 = 9{,}216\)

36× fewer

[0:20 · 3 min] The hypothesis behind LoRA (Hu et al.) is that the change needed to adapt a model has low "intrinsic rank": a few directions are enough. Walk through the zero initialisation of B: at step 0 the model is exactly the pretrained one. After training, W0 + (alpha/r)·BA can be merged into a single matrix, so inference costs the same as the base model. In PEFT, target_modules says which matrices get an update; in SmolLM2 they are called q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj and the MLP gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj.

From base model to assistant

Pretraining → SFT → preference tuning

Pretraining

Next-token prediction on huge unlabelled text. Knows language, not how to follow requests.

→

Supervised fine-tuning

Instruction → ideal response pairs written or checked by people. Learns to follow instructions and a format.

→

Preference tuning

People rank several answers; the model is optimised towards preferred ones (RLHF).

InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022): labelers preferred the outputs of a 1.3 B instruction-tuned model over those of the 175 B GPT-3, despite 100× fewer parameters.

SmolLM2-135M-Instruct already went through these steps. Today we add one more, small SFT step for a brand voice.

Loss masking: labels = −100 for the system and user tokens, so the model learns to write answers, not to predict questions.
[0:33 · 3 min] Always format fine-tuning data with the same chat template the model uses at inference: tokenizer.apply_chat_template does it. A mismatch (different tags, missing end token) is one of the most common silent bugs. In PyTorch, label −100 is ignored by the cross-entropy loss. Keep the end-of-turn token in the labels: that is how the model learns to stop.

Building a custom dataset

Five steps, quality over quantity

1 · Collect

Real inputs: tickets, chats, emails. Cover rare but important cases.

2 · Define

Labelling guidelines: definitions, examples, edge cases, "other".

3 · Label

Two people label a sample; measure agreement (e.g., Cohen's κ) and fix the guide.

4 · Split

Stratified train / validation / test. No duplicates across splits; split by customer or date if needed.

5 · Protect

Remove personal data (names, IDs, account numbers) before labelling or training.

Quality > quantity: LIMA fine-tuned a 65 B model on only 1,000 carefully curated examples (Zhou et al., 2023). Noisy labels teach noise.
Colombia: personal data in training sets falls under Ley 1581 de 2012. Document your dataset with a datasheet (Gebru et al., 2021).
[0:36 · 3 min] Step 3 is the most skipped: if two careful humans disagree on 20% of labels, no model will reach 95% accuracy against either of them. Leakage in step 4: near-duplicate messages from the same customer in train and test inflate scores. The test set is used once, at the end. Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al.) is a template for documenting motivation, composition, collection process and recommended uses; the project's model card builds on the same idea.

[0:50 · 3 min] Starting points, not laws. The learning rate is the most sensitive knob: too high and the pretrained knowledge is destroyed within a few steps; too low and nothing happens in your budget. AdamW decouples weight decay from the gradient update (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019). LoRA tolerates and usually needs higher learning rates than full fine-tuning because far fewer weights move; in today's lab we use 1e-3 for LoRA and 5e-5 for DistilBERT.

Reading learning curves

Four shapes you will meet

📉 Underfitting

Training and validation loss both still high and falling when training stops.

Fix: more epochs, higher learning rate, bigger model or LoRA rank.

✅ Good fit

Validation loss reaches its minimum and flattens; small gap to training loss.

Keep the checkpoint with the best validation score.

📈 Overfitting

Training loss keeps falling while validation loss turns up.

Fix: fewer epochs, early stopping, more data, weight decay, smaller r.

💥 Divergence

Loss jumps, oscillates or becomes NaN; accuracy near chance.

Fix: lower the learning rate, add warmup, clip gradients.

[0:53 · 2 min] Quick tour; the simulator on the next slide shows each shape. For a 10-class problem, the initial loss of a random classifier head is about ln(10) ≈ 2.3: a loss that stays around 2.3 means the model learned nothing (often after divergence it predicts a single class).

Example-passes = epochs × examples: the compute you paid for.

Small data

Overfitting: memorising 200 examples is easy

A 67 M-parameter model can memorise a few hundred sentences perfectly. Training accuracy then says nothing; only held-out data does.

  • Evaluate every epoch on validation (eval_strategy="epoch").
  • Early stopping: keep the best validation checkpoint.
  • Regularise: weight decay, dropout, fewer epochs, PEFT with a small r.
  • More or better data beats clever tricks.
  • Tiny validation sets are noisy: with 50 examples, one error moves accuracy by 2 points.

Catastrophic forgetting

Learning task B can erase task A

Fine-tuning pushes weights towards the new data; skills that are not in that data can degrade. Elastic weight consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017) penalises changes to weights that mattered for the old task:

$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_B(\theta) + \sum_i \frac{\lambda}{2}\, F_i \big(\theta_i - \theta^{*}_{A,i}\big)^2 $$

\(F_i\): Fisher information (importance of weight \(i\) for task A) · \(\lambda\): how much to protect A.

Practical defences

  • Small learning rate and few epochs.
  • PEFT: the base stays frozen; switch the adapter off to recover it.
  • Mix some general examples into the training data (replay).
  • Run a "general skills" test set before and after every fine-tune.
[1:00 · 2 min] Intuition for EWC: some weights are important for old skills (high Fisher information), so we tie them to their old values with a spring whose stiffness is F_i. Most practitioners do not implement EWC; they use the practical defences on the right. In the lab, students ask the LoRA model a general question to check for side effects, and switch the adapter off to show that the base model is untouched.

Side effects are a business risk

Re-test everything after every fine-tune

NovaTel (fictional) fine-tunes its support model on 500 friendly conversations. Tone improves, but nobody re-runs the safety tests: the model now also answers requests it used to refuse.

Qi et al. (2024) showed that fine-tuning aligned models, even on benign data, can weaken their safety behaviour.

A release checklist for fine-tuned models

  • Task metrics on the test set (accuracy, F1, human rating).
  • General skills and safety prompts: same answers as the base model?
  • Prompts in Spanish and English, short and long.
  • Model card: data, hyperparameters, known limits (S10).
[1:03 · 1 min] Business lens for Part 3. Reference: Qi, Zeng, Xie, Chen, Jia, Mittal and Henderson, "Fine-tuning aligned language models compromises safety, even when users do not intend to!" (ICLR 2024). The fix is procedural: a fixed regression suite that runs before every release.

Decision guide

Climb the ladder only when the evidence says so

1 · Prompting

Clear instructions + a few examples + an evaluation set of real cases.

Always the baseline. Stop here if it passes your evaluation.

2 · RAG

Failures come from missing or changing knowledge: policies, prices, product manuals.

Retrieve the facts at question time; update documents, not weights.

3 · Fine-tuning

Failures come from behaviour: format, tone, a narrow classification, jargon, or cost and latency at scale.

Needs labelled examples, evaluation and maintenance.

They combine: fine-tune for how to answer, RAG for what is true today. Fine-tuning is a poor way to teach facts that change every month.
[1:04 · 2 min] Same ladder as the adaptation spectrum in S01, now with diagnostic questions. OpenAI's optimisation guide describes the same loop: write evals first, try prompting, fine-tune only when evals show a need, and repeat. Classification with many labelled examples (Banking77) is a textbook case for fine-tuning a small encoder: cheaper and faster than prompting a large LLM.

Wrap-up

Key takeaways

  • Full fine-tuning trains every weight (≈16 bytes per parameter); PEFT trains a tiny fraction.
  • LoRA: \(W = W_0 + BA\), rank \(r\) sets capacity; adapters are small, swappable and mergeable.
  • Choose with validation, report with test once; read the curves before trusting a number.
  • Prompt first, RAG for changing knowledge, fine-tune for behaviour, and re-test after every fine-tune.
