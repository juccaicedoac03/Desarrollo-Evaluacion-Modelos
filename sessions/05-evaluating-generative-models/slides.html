Warm-up · Q1

With LoRA, a weight matrix becomes \(W = W_0 + BA\). During fine-tuning, what is trained?

\(B\) is \(d \times r\) and \(A\) is \(r \times k\) with a small rank \(r\), so each adapted matrix trains \(r(d + k)\) parameters instead of \(d \cdot k\). The pretrained \(W_0\) is untouched, which is why adapters are small files and several can share one base model.
[0:03 · 2 min] Poll, then click. If people pick (a), recall the LoRA calculator from S04: with r = 8 on a 768 × 768 matrix, 12,288 trainable parameters instead of 589,824.
Warm-up · Q2

Fine-tuning on 200 examples: training loss keeps falling, but validation loss rises after epoch 2. What is happening?

The model is memorising the 200 training examples. Early stopping on the validation loss, fewer epochs, weight decay, LoRA dropout or more (and more varied) data all help. Today we make "validation" precise: how to split, how many folds, and what to keep sealed.

Part 1

Why is generation hard to evaluate?

Many right answers, many ways to be wrong, and a model's own measure of surprise.

Theory · 60'

The core difficulty

One input, many right answers

Input: a 6-minute call in which an Andes Bank customer reports a card charged twice at a supermarket and asks for a refund.

Three good summaries

  1. Customer reports a duplicate supermarket charge and requests a refund.
  2. Double card payment at a supermarket; refund requested.
  3. Client was billed twice for one purchase and wants the money back.

One fluent, wrong summary

No single reference: good outputs differ in words and order.
Many dimensions: fluency, faithfulness, coverage, tone, safety.
Humans disagree too, and their time is expensive.

A map of evaluation

Intrinsic vs extrinsic evaluation

Intrinsic: the output itself

  • Reference-free: perplexity, an LLM judge, human ratings
  • Lexical overlap with a reference: BLEU, ROUGE, chrF
  • Semantic similarity to a reference: embeddings, BERTScore
  • Task metrics for labels: precision, recall, F1

Extrinsic: the effect it causes

  • Did the agent resolve the case faster?
  • Did fewer customers call back?
  • Did fraud losses or review hours go down?
  • Measured offline (a simulation, a pilot) or online (A/B test, shadow mode)
Rule of thumb: intrinsic metrics are cheap and fast, so you use them to iterate; extrinsic ones are what the business pays for, so you use them to decide.
[0:13 · 2 min] Intrinsic = judged in isolation; extrinsic = judged by its impact on a downstream task or on people. Most of today's lab is intrinsic; the breakout and the challenge's "Explica y decide" connect it to extrinsic KPIs. Shadow mode: the model runs on real traffic but its outputs are logged, not shown, so you can compare with what humans did.

Reference-free · perplexity

Perplexity: how surprised is the model?

For a text of \(N\) tokens, perplexity is the exponential of the average negative log-likelihood:

$$ \text{PPL} = \exp\Big(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \log p(x_t \mid x_{<t})\Big) $$

Worked example

The model gives the three tokens probabilities \(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18\).

Mean NLL \(= \tfrac13(\ln 2 + \ln 4 + \ln 8) = \ln 4\) → \(\text{PPL} = 4\).

As uncertain as a fair choice among 4 tokens at every step.

What it is good for

  • Tracking training and validation loss (it is \(e^{\text{loss}}\))
  • Comparing models that share a tokenizer on the same text
  • Spotting out-of-domain text (tweets, another language)

Introduced for speech recognition by Jelinek et al. (1977); see Jurafsky & Martin, SLP3, ch. 3.

[0:15 · 2 min] Walk the worked example: the geometric mean of 2, 4 and 8 is 4. Lower is better, the minimum is 1 (perfect certainty). Connect to S02 and S04: the cross-entropy loss printed by Trainer is the mean NLL, so exp(loss) is the perplexity. It needs no reference text, which is its strength and its limit: it says how fluent a text is under the model, not whether it is true.

Buying on benchmarks

A leaderboard is not your test set

Reference-based · translation

BLEU: clipped n-gram precision + brevity penalty

1 · Clipped precision \(p_n\): each candidate n-gram counts at most as many times as it appears in the reference.

2 · Brevity penalty for a candidate of length \(c\) and reference length \(r\):

$$ \text{BP} = \begin{cases} 1 & c > r \\ e^{\,1 - r/c} & c \le r \end{cases} $$

3 · Geometric mean of \(p_1 \dots p_4\):

$$ \text{BLEU} = \text{BP} \cdot \exp\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{4} \tfrac14 \log p_n\Big) $$

Why clip? (Papineni et al., 2002)

Reference: the cat is on the mat
Candidate: the the the the the the the

Plain unigram precision: 7/7. Clipped: 2/7, because "the" appears only twice in the reference.

BLEU is precision-based; recall is only approximated by the brevity penalty. Corpus BLEU pools counts over all sentences.

[0:23 · 3 min] BLEU asks "how much of what the system wrote is supported by the reference?" That is precision. Without the brevity penalty, a one-word output made of a correct word would get a perfect score; BP is the patch that punishes short outputs. With several references, a candidate n-gram is clipped by its maximum count in any single reference. sacrebleu reports BLEU on a 0–100 scale together with a signature (tokenizer, smoothing) so scores are reproducible (Post, 2018).

Reference-based · summaries and morphology

ROUGE looks at recall, chrF at characters

ROUGE (Lin, 2004)

ROUGE-N recall: the share of the reference's n-grams that the candidate covers (ROUGE-1 unigrams, ROUGE-2 bigrams).

ROUGE-L: longest common subsequence (LCS) of \(m\) reference and \(n\) candidate words; order matters, gaps are allowed.

\(R = \tfrac{\text{LCS}}{m}\) · \(P = \tfrac{\text{LCS}}{n}\) · \(F_1 = \tfrac{2PR}{P + R}\) (as in the rouge_score package)

chrF (Popović, 2015)

An F-score over character n-grams (up to 6 characters). Partial credit for aprobó vs aprobado, which share most characters.

\(F_\beta\) with \(\beta = 2\) in sacrebleu: recall counts more than precision.

Useful for morphologically rich languages such as Spanish.

Shared blind spot: all three reward surface overlap with one reference. None of them checks that the facts are right.
[0:26 · 2 min] ROUGE was designed for summaries, where missing key content is the main failure, hence recall. ROUGE-L rewards words in the same order without requiring them to be contiguous. chrF works below the word level, so it gives partial credit to inflected forms, frequent in Spanish. Reference-based · meaning

Embedding similarity and BERTScore

  • Sentence embeddings (e.g., all-MiniLM-L6-v2; Reimers & Gurevych, 2019): one vector per text; cosine similarity near 1 means "about the same thing".
  • BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020): match every token to its most similar token in the other text using contextual embeddings, then compute precision, recall and F1.
  • Both reward paraphrases that n-gram metrics miss.

The reference standard

Human evaluation, designed properly

What to rate

Explicit criteria: fluency, faithfulness to the source, coverage, tone, safety. One question per criterion.

How to rate

Likert (1–5 per criterion) or pairwise (A vs B, which is better?). Pairwise is often easier for raters to answer consistently.

Who and how many

At least two raters on a shared sample; measure agreement (e.g., Cohen's κ); blind them to which system wrote what.

Guidelines with examples are the cheapest way to raise agreement: show a 1, a 3 and a 5 for each criterion before rating starts.
[0:36 · 2 min] Human evaluation is the reference standard, but only if it is designed: criteria, scale, raters, agreement. Low agreement usually means unclear guidelines, not bad raters. Randomise the order of systems and hide their names to avoid bias. This connects to the labelling-guidelines breakout from S04.

Scaling human judgment

LLM-as-a-judge: useful, but biased

A strong LLM grades one answer against a rubric, or compares two answers. Zheng et al. (2023) found that strong judges such as GPT-4 agreed with human preferences more than 80% of the time on their benchmarks, similar to the agreement between humans.

Known biases

  • Position: prefers the answer in a given slot
  • Verbosity: prefers longer answers
  • Self-enhancement: favours answers from its own model family
  • Limited reasoning when grading maths or logic

Mitigations

  • Swap A and B; accept a verdict only if both orders agree
  • Give a rubric and, when possible, a reference answer
  • Ask for a justification before the verdict
  • Use a judge from a different family than the system under test
  • Validate the judge on a sample with human ratings before trusting it
[0:38 · 2 min] The paper introduced MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena; its agreement figures are for those settings, not a guarantee for yours. A judge is a model, so it needs its own evaluation: compare it with a few dozen human ratings on your data. In the lab there is a ready-made judge prompt students can paste into any assistant, swapping A and B to see position bias.

Classification metrics

The confusion matrix and its ratios

FlaggedNot flagged
Actually positiveTPFN
Actually negativeFPTN

\(\text{Precision} = \dfrac{TP}{TP + FP}\) · \(\text{Recall} = \dfrac{TP}{TP + FN}\)

\(F_1 = \dfrac{2 \cdot P \cdot R}{P + R}\) · \(\text{Accuracy} = \dfrac{TP + TN}{\text{all}}\)

  • Precision: of what we flagged, how much was right? (cost of false alarms)
  • Recall: of what was there, how much did we catch? (cost of misses)
  • Accuracy paradox: if 1% of transactions are fraud, "never fraud" is 99% accurate with 0 recall.
  • Several classes: macro-F1 averages classes equally (small classes count); weighted-F1 follows class frequency.

Reference: Google, Machine Learning Crash Course, classification module.

[0:44 · 2 min] Keep it concrete: precision answers the analyst ("how many of my alerts are real?"), recall answers the risk manager ("how much fraud got through?"). F1 is the harmonic mean, so it is low if either is low. With imbalance, never report accuracy alone. In the lab we use macro-F1 over 10 intents so that every intent matters equally.

Validation

Holdout, k-fold and the sealed test set

Stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training data:

Each fold keeps the class proportions (stratification). Report the mean and the spread across folds.

  • Holdout: one train/validation/test split. Simple, but one split can be lucky or unlucky.
  • k-fold: every example is validated once; use it to choose models and hyperparameters when data are scarce.
  • Test set: touched once, at the end, to confirm. If you tune on cross-validation, the CV score is optimistic: keep the test set (or use nested CV).

Kohavi (1995) recommended stratified 10-fold cross-validation for model selection.

[0:52 · 2 min] The grid shows five rounds: each round trains on four folds and validates on the fifth. The test set stays sealed throughout. In the lab, students see that single 80/20 splits can differ by several F1 points, while 5-fold gives a mean plus a standard deviation. Kohavi's 1995 study compared cross-validation and bootstrap and recommended stratified 10-fold for model selection. Challenge: each student gets k = 3, 5 or 10.

From numbers to fixes

Error analysis: read the mistakes

1 · Sample

20+ errors from the validation set, or the top confused pairs.

2 · Group

Ambiguous input, overlapping labels, missing vocabulary, label noise…

3 · Count

How many errors per group? Fix the biggest group first.

4 · Hypothesise

One cause and one change per group.

5 · Re-evaluate

On validation (CV); confirm on test once.

In today's lab (Banking77, 10 intents)

Top confusions include pending_transfer → transfer_not_received_by_recipient: "Why hasn't my transfer gone through yet?" could be either. That is a label-definition problem more than a model problem.

Slices: also break the metric down by segment (channel, language, product, customer type). An average can hide a group where the model fails.
[0:57 · 1 min] This is the workflow the final project requires (at least 20 errors, grouped and counted). The lab example comes from the confusion matrix students will compute in Part 4. Some errors are best fixed by merging or redefining labels, not by a bigger model.

Wrap-up

Key takeaways

  • Perplexity measures surprise under a model; compare it only with the same text and tokenizer.
  • BLEU is precision with a brevity penalty, ROUGE is recall-oriented, chrF works on characters: all measure overlap, not truth.
  • Embeddings, human raters and validated LLM judges add meaning, at a cost and with biases.
  • Choose the threshold with explicit costs; report k-fold mean ± spread; open the test set once.
  • Every metric should point to a KPI and a go/no-go rule written in advance.
