Warm-up · Q1

You lower the sampling temperature from 1.0 to 0.2. What happens to the next-token distribution?

Softmax divides the logits by \(T\) before normalising: with \(T < 1\) the differences between logits grow, so the top tokens take almost all the probability. Temperature changes decoding, never the weights.

Part 1

The training loop

Losses, gradients, learning rates and optimizers: the engine under every model you will train.

Theory · 60'
[0:10] Theory block: three parts of about 20 minutes, with a quiz, a simulator or a breakout roughly every 8 minutes.

What does “training” mean?

Search for weights that make the loss small

A model is a function \(f_\theta\) with parameters \(\theta\) (the weights). A loss measures how wrong it is on the training data:

$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell\big(f_\theta(x_i),\, y_i\big) \qquad \text{training:}\ \ \theta^\star \approx \arg\min_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta) $$

mini-batch → 1 · forward → 2 · loss → 3 · backward (gradients) → 4 · step (update θ) ↺

Generative twist

The “label” is often the data itself: the next token for a language model, the input image for an autoencoder. That is why unlabelled data is enough (self-supervision).

Vocabulary

Step (iteration): one mini-batch update. Epoch: one full pass over the training set. 20,000 images in batches of 128 ≈ 157 steps per epoch.

[0:10 · 2 min] The goal is not the minimum on training data per se: we want low loss on data the model has never seen (generalization, Part 2). Arithmetic for the step count: 20,000 / 128 = 156.25, so 157 mini-batches with the last one smaller. Goodfellow et al. (2016), chapter 8, is the reference for this part.

The loop in PyTorch

Five lines you will write in every project


model = VAE(latent_dim=8).to(DEVICE)
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
for epoch in range(EPOCHS):
    model.train()                        # training mode: dropout on
    for x, _ in train_loader:            # one mini-batch at a time
        x = x.to(DEVICE)
        optimizer.zero_grad()            # 1 · clear the previous gradients
        logits, mu, logvar = model(x)    # 2 · forward pass
        loss = vae_loss(logits, x, mu, logvar)   # 3 · loss on this batch
        loss.backward()                  # 4 · backward: gradients for every weight
        optimizer.step()                 # 5 · update the weights
    val_loss = evaluate(model, val_loader)       # 6 · check unseen data

evaluate() switches to model.eval() and torch.no_grad(): no dropout, no gradients. Forget zero_grad() and gradients from old batches pile up.

[0:12 · 3 min] Press the right arrow to step through the highlights: first the five core lines, then the train/eval modes. This loop is the same for a VAE, a classifier or GPT-2; only the model and the loss change. The Hugging Face Trainer (Session 04) runs exactly this loop for you. Plant the seed: in today's challenge an "AI assistant" wrote this loop with three bugs.

Loss functions

Three losses you will meet all course long

LossFormulaUse it when…In generative models
Cross-entropy\(-\sum_k y_k \log p_k\)The target is a category (a class, a token)Next-token prediction in every LLM; binary cross-entropy for pixels in [0, 1]
MSE\(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\)The target is a continuous valueReconstructing real-valued data; diffusion models predict the added noise with MSE
KL divergence\(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p) = \sum_z q(z) \log \frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\)You compare two probability distributionsThe VAE regulariser; keeping a fine-tuned model close to the original one
 KL is always ≥ 0 and equals 0 only when \(q = p\). It is not symmetric: \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p) \neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\) in general.
Key idea: minimising cross-entropy is the same as maximising the likelihood of the training data (maximum likelihood).
[0:15 · 3 min] Keep it intuitive. Cross-entropy punishes putting low probability on what actually happened. MSE punishes squared distance. KL measures how much one distribution differs from a reference one. Diffusion: Ho et al. (2020) train with a simple MSE on the noise. Remember "KL is never negative": if your logs show a negative KL, you have a sign bug.
Check · losses

A language model is pretrained to predict the next token among 50,000 possible tokens. Which loss fits?

Token ids are categories, not quantities: token 812 is not “closer” to 813 than to 40,000. Cross-entropy on the softmax output is the standard choice. Accuracy is a useful metric, but it is not differentiable, so it cannot drive gradient descent.
[0:18 · 2 min] Poll, then click. Distinguish loss (what we optimise, must be differentiable) from metric (what we report to the business). They often differ; Session 05 is all about metrics.

Gradient descent

Walk downhill, one step at a time

$$ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t) $$

The gradient points uphill; we step the other way. The learning rate \(\eta\) sets the step size.

🐢 Too small

Safe but slow: many epochs, more compute, and it may stop in the first valley it finds.

✅ About right

The loss falls quickly and then settles near a minimum.

💥 Too large

Steps overshoot the valley: the loss oscillates, or grows until it becomes inf or nan.

Stochastic gradient descent estimates the gradient on a mini-batch: noisier, but far cheaper than using all the data at every step.

[0:20 · 1.5 min] Real losses have millions of dimensions, but the intuition from one dimension transfers. Next slide: try it yourself. The noise of mini-batches is not only a cost; it also helps escape small bumps.

Optimizers

From plain SGD to AdamW

OptimizerUpdate ideaIn practice
SGD\(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, g\)Simple and well understood; needs a well-tuned learning rate and schedule
SGD + momentum\(v \leftarrow \mu v + g,\ \ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, v\)A “heavy ball” (Polyak, 1964): smooths zig-zags and rolls past small bumps
AdamStep per weight from running averages of \(g\) and \(g^2\)Robust default; the paper's default rate is \(10^{-3}\) (Kingma & Ba, 2015)
AdamWAdam + weight decay applied directly to the weightsStandard for Transformers and fine-tuning (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019)
 \(g\) = gradient of the loss on the current mini-batch; \(\mu\) = momentum coefficient (often 0.9).

Adam adapts the step size of every weight separately, which is why it usually makes progress faster than SGD with the same learning rate. In the lab you will compare both for one epoch.

[0:26 · 2 min] No need to derive Adam. Intuition: weights with consistently small gradients get relatively bigger steps, and noisy ones get smaller steps. AdamW fixes how weight decay interacts with Adam: "decoupled" means the decay is applied to the weights directly instead of being added to the gradient. It is the default optimizer in the Hugging Face Trainer.

Learning-rate schedules and batch size

The learning rate does not have to stay fixed

  • Warm-up: start small for the first steps, while the optimizer's statistics are still unreliable.
  • Decay: big steps early to make progress, small steps late to settle. Cosine decay: Loshchilov & Hutter (2017).
  • Batch size: larger batches give smoother gradients and use the GPU better, but need more memory and give fewer steps per epoch. If you change it a lot, re-tune the learning rate.
Start from known defaults: Adam at \(10^{-3}\) for small models trained from scratch; smaller rates for fine-tuning (Session 04).
[0:28 · 1.5 min] The chart is illustrative: learning rate as a fraction of its peak value over 100 steps. Warm-up plus cosine decay is common for Transformers. Do not spend long here; the point is that the learning rate is a schedule, not a single number.

Generalization and data

Overfitting, regularization, early stopping, and the data mistakes that no optimizer can fix.

[0:31] Part 2: a low training loss is not the goal; good behaviour on new data is.

Generalization

Underfitting, good fit, overfitting

Underfitting

Training and validation loss both stay high. The model is too simple or trained too little.

Good fit

Both losses are low and close to each other; validation loss is flat near its minimum.

Overfitting

Training loss keeps falling while validation loss rises: the model memorises the training set.

SplitUsed to…Typical share
TrainFit the weights70–80%
ValidationChoose hyperparameters and when to stop10–15%
TestEstimate final performance, once, at the end10–15%
 Shares are common conventions, not rules. If you tune on the test set, it stops being a test.
[0:31 · 2 min] The validation set is what we look at while developing; the test set is a sealed envelope. Fashion-MNIST ships a fixed train/test split (60,000 / 10,000); in the lab we use part of the test split as validation for speed, and say so explicitly.

Regularization

Tools to close the gap

Weight decay

Penalise large weights (L2). In AdamW, the weight_decay argument.

Dropout

Randomly switch off units during training only (Srivastava et al., 2014).

More or augmented data

Often the strongest regulariser, when you can get it.

Smaller model

Less capacity to memorise. Fewer parameters also run cheaper.

Early stopping

Watch validation loss; keep the best checkpoint; stop after patience epochs without improvement.


if val_loss < best - 1e-4:
    best, bad = val_loss, 0
    save_checkpoint(model)
else:
    bad += 1
if bad >= patience:
    break
[0:37 · 2 min] Dropout is active only in model.train() mode: this is why forgetting to switch modes matters (you will see it in the challenge). Early stopping is also a cost saver: it ends runs that are no longer improving.

Data preprocessing

Most training problems start in the data

1 · Clean

Duplicates, missing values, impossible values, inconsistent formats and units.

2 · Split

Before any fitting. By customer or by time when rows are related.

3 · Normalise

Fit scalers on train only, then apply them to validation and test. Pixels: divide by 255 → [0, 1].

4 · Encode

Text → tokens (S01); categories → ids or one-hot; images → tensors.

Why normalise? Features on very different scales (age vs. balance in pesos) make gradients unbalanced and training slow or unstable.
Order matters: computing a mean, a vocabulary or a scaler on all the data before splitting leaks test information into training.
[0:40 · 2 min] Split early is the key habit. In the lab the pixels are divided by 255: this is a fixed transform, not fitted on data, so it cannot leak. A z-score scaler, by contrast, learns a mean and a standard deviation, so it must be fitted on training data only.

Data leakage

When the model sees the answer in advance

Leakage: information is available during training that will not be available when the model is used (Kaufman et al., 2012).

TypeExample in a CRMFix
Target leakageA “cancellation call date” feature, recorded only after the customer asked to leaveKeep only fields that exist at prediction time
Train–test contaminationThe same customer appears in both sets; a scaler fitted on all rowsSplit by customer; fit preprocessing on train only
Temporal leakageA random split of monthly snapshots: the model trains on 2024 to “predict” 2023Split by time: train on the past, validate on the future
Symptom: validation results that look too good to be true, then collapse in production.
[0:42 · 2 min] Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich and Stitelman (2012) is the classic reference; they describe leakage in data-mining competitions and industry. The rule of thumb: for every feature, ask "would I know this value at the moment I make the prediction?"

[0:44 · 6 min + 2 min debrief] Rooms of 3–4, mixed profiles. Andes Bank is fictional. Debrief (there are six; five is enough): 1) income = 0 confuses "unknown" with "no income": impute with a statistic fitted on train and add a "missing" flag. 2) Scaler fitted on all rows: contamination; fit on train only. 3) Random row split: the same customer lands in both sets and future months train the model for past months; split by customer and by time. 4) retention_call_date exists only once the bank already knows the customer is leaving: target leakage; drop it. 5) Names, ID numbers and raw complaint text sent to a generator: personal data (Ley 1581 de 2012), remove or pseudonymise and minimise. 6) 97% accuracy is what "nobody churns" gets with a 3% churn rate; report recall, precision or PR-AUC and compare with a trivial baseline.

Decisions built on leaks

A 97% model that catches nobody

Andes Bank (fictional) funds a retention campaign because the churn model “scored 97%”. In production it flags almost no one who actually leaves: the score came from leaked fields and a trivial baseline.

The cost is not the model: it is the campaign budget, the lost customers and the lost trust in data projects.

Questions a manager should ask

  • Which fields exist at the moment of prediction?
  • How was the split made: by customer? by time?
  • What does a trivial baseline score on the same data?
  • Was the test set used only once, at the end?

Part 3

From autoencoders to VAEs

A latent space you can sample from: the model you will train in the lab.

[0:53] Part 3: the variational autoencoder, step by step, then how it compares to GANs and diffusion.

Autoencoders

Compress, then rebuild

x · 784 pixels → encoder → z · 8 numbers → decoder → x̂ · 784 pixels

What it learns

Trained to make \(\hat{x} \approx x\) through a narrow bottleneck, the network must keep only the essential features.

Uses: compression, denoising, anomaly detection (unusual inputs reconstruct badly).

Why it is not (yet) a good generator

Nothing organises the latent space: codes can sit anywhere, with gaps between them. Decode a random \(z\) and you often get noise.

Goodfellow et al. (2016), ch. 14.

[0:53 · 1.5 min] 784 = 28 × 28 pixels, the Fashion-MNIST image size. The bottleneck of 8 numbers is today's lab setting. Anomaly detection is a real business use: fraud or defect detection by reconstruction error.

Variational autoencoder

Encode to a distribution, not to a point

x → encoder → μ(x), σ(x) → z = μ + σ ⊙ ε → decoder → x̂

Encoder \(q_\phi(z \mid x)\)

Outputs a Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\mu(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2(x))\) for each input: a small cloud in latent space.

Prior \(p(z)\)

A standard normal \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\). Training pulls every cloud towards it, so the space has no gaps.

Decoder \(p_\theta(x \mid z)\)

Turns a latent code into data. To generate: sample \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and decode it.

Kingma & Welling (2014); a longer tutorial is Kingma & Welling (2019).

[0:55 · 2 min] The key change from a plain autoencoder: each input maps to a small region, not a point, and all regions are pulled towards the same standard normal. Result: nearby codes decode to similar images, and random codes from N(0, I) decode to plausible images.

The training objective

ELBO = reconstruction − KL

$$ \log p_\theta(x) \;\ge\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regulariser}} \;=\; \text{ELBO} $$

Loss = −ELBO = recon + KL

Reconstruction: binary cross-entropy summed over the 784 pixels (pixel values in [0, 1]).

KL for Gaussians, in closed form:

\( D_{\mathrm{KL}} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(1 + \log\sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2\big) \)

A tug of war

Reconstruction wants precise, well-separated codes. KL wants every code close to \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\). Training finds a balance.

Sanity check: KL ≥ 0 always. A negative KL in your logs means a sign error.
[0:57 · 3 min] ELBO = evidence lower bound: a lower bound on the log-likelihood of the data. Maximising it pushes the likelihood up. We minimise its negative. Per image we sum over pixels and over latent dimensions, then average over the batch. Point out the d in the KL sum: the latent dimension, 8 in the lab. The sign check is exactly one of the bugs in the challenge.

The reparameterization trick

Move the randomness out of the way

We cannot back-propagate through “draw \(z\) at random from \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\)”. Rewrite the sample as a deterministic function plus independent noise:

$$ z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) $$ 

def reparameterize(mu, logvar):
    std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)  # σ
    eps = torch.randn_like(std)    # ε ~ N(0, I)
    return mu + std * eps          # z = μ + σ ⊙ ε

The randomness now lives in \(\varepsilon\); gradients flow through \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) back to the encoder.

The encoder predicts \(\log \sigma^2\) (any real number) instead of \(\sigma\) (must be positive), and \(\sigma = \exp(\tfrac{1}{2}\log \sigma^2)\): simpler and numerically stable.

[1:00 · 2 min] Kingma and Welling's key technical contribution. Analogy for non-technical participants: instead of asking "which random number came out?", we ask "how far from the centre, in units of spread?" and learn the centre and the spread.

β-VAE

One knob for the trade-off

$$ \mathcal{L}_\beta = \text{recon} + \beta \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}} \qquad \text{(Higgins et al., 2017)} $$
SettingReconstructionLatent spaceSamples from the prior
\(\beta < 1\)SharperLess regular, can have gapsMore often implausible
\(\beta = 1\)The standard VAE: the loss is exactly −ELBO
\(\beta > 1\)BlurrierSmoother; fewer active dimensionsSmoother, less detailed
Compare models with the same yardstick: report reconstruction and KL separately, plus recon + KL (−ELBO with β = 1). The β-weighted loss is not comparable across different β.
[1:02 · 2 min] Higgins and colleagues showed that β above 1 encourages disentangled latent factors. An "active" latent dimension is one whose mean actually varies with the input; with a strong KL weight, some dimensions give up and simply copy the prior. The latent size interacts with β: more dimensions allow better reconstruction, but not all of them will be used. In the challenge, each student gets a β and a latent size and compares against β = 1, latent 8.

Self-check · define it before you flip it

VAE vocabulary

Reconstruction term
How well the decoder rebuilds \(x\) from \(z\); here binary cross-entropy summed over pixels.
KL term
How far each encoded Gaussian is from the prior \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\). Never negative.
Reparameterization
\(z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon\): sampling rewritten so gradients reach the encoder.
Sampling from the prior
Draw \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and decode it: a brand-new example.
Latent interpolation
Decode points on the line between two codes: one garment morphs into another.
Active dimension
A latent dimension whose mean changes with the input; inactive ones just copy the prior.

Check · VAEs

Your VAE reconstructs test images well, but images decoded from random \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) look like noise. Which change is most likely to help?

Good reconstructions with poor samples mean the codes the decoder learned sit far from the prior: the KL term is too weak. A larger β pulls the codes towards \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\), where we sample, at some cost in sharpness. β = 0 is a plain autoencoder: the worst option for sampling.
[1:05 · 1.5 min] Poll, then click. This is the reconstruction-versus-KL trade-off that students must interpret with their own numbers in challenge task 2.

Wrap-up

Key takeaways

  • Every model trains with the same loop: forward, loss, backward, step.
  • The learning rate is the first knob to check when a loss curve looks wrong.
  • Judge a model on validation data; stop early, regularise, and hunt for leakage.
  • A VAE balances reconstruction against KL; β moves the balance.
