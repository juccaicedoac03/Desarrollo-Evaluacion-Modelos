Warm-up · Q1

Dell'Acqua et al. (2023) gave consultants GPT-4 for a task designed to fall outside the AI's capability frontier. What happened?

The "jagged technological frontier": inside it, AI users were faster and produced higher-quality work; on the task outside it, they were about 19 percentage points less likely to be correct. Fluent output invites over-reliance, which is an ethical risk as much as a quality one.
[0:02 · 3 min] Poll first, then click. Link to today: over-reliance means people stop checking. That is why human oversight in the EU AI Act is about effective oversight (people who can understand and override the system), not a person who rubber-stamps outputs.
Warm-up · Q2

In Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024), the airline's website chatbot gave a customer wrong information about a fare policy. What did the tribunal decide?

The British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal rejected the idea that the chatbot was a separate entity and found the airline liable for negligent misrepresentation. Deploying a model means owning what it says.

Risk taxonomy

Eight families of risk

⚖️ Bias

Unequal quality or stereotypes for groups of people.

A CV ranker that scores some names lower.

🔒 Privacy

Personal data memorised, leaked or re-identified.

A model that repeats a phone number from training data.

🌀 Hallucination

Fluent, confident and false.

An invented refund policy (S01, S09).

©️ IP & copyright

Rights over training data and outputs; model licences.

Outputs that reproduce a protected text.

🛡️ Security

Prompt injection, data poisoning, data exfiltration.

A web page that hijacks an agent.

🎭 Misuse

Fraud, phishing, disinformation, deepfakes.

A cloned voice asking for a transfer.

🌍 Environment

Energy, emissions, water and hardware.

Millions of requests on a carbon-heavy grid.

👷 Labour

Task displacement, deskilling, hidden annotation work.

Agents who only rubber-stamp outputs.

Two papers every AI team should read

Stochastic parrots and a taxonomy of harms

Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major & Shmitchell (2021)

On the dangers of stochastic parrots: Can language models be too big? (FAccT)

  • Environmental and financial costs grow with scale, and are not paid by those who benefit.
  • Web-scale data is too large to document ("documentation debt") and over-represents dominant views.
  • Fluent text is easily mistaken for understanding.
  • Recommendation: weigh costs first, curate and document data, run pre-mortems.

Weidinger et al. (2021)

Ethical and social risks of harm from language models (arXiv:2112.04359)

  1. Discrimination, exclusion and toxicity
  2. Information hazards (e.g., private data leaks)
  3. Misinformation harms
  4. Malicious uses
  5. Human–computer interaction harms
  6. Automation, access and environmental harms
[0:13 · 3 min] Bender et al. is one of the most cited critical papers on large language models; its four authors are women, and it is one of today's assigned readings. Weidinger et al. offers a structured list of 21 risks in six areas; use it as a checklist for the project's ethics section.

Security

When the input is the attack

Prompt injection

Direct: the user tries to override the instructions. Indirect: instructions hidden in a web page, email or file that the model reads (Greshake et al., 2023).

Riskier with tools and agents (S07).

Data poisoning

An attacker plants examples in training, fine-tuning or RAG data to create a backdoor or bias. Poisoning web-scale data has been shown to be practical (Carlini et al., 2024).

Training-data extraction

Carlini et al. (2021) extracted verbatim training text from GPT-2, including names, phone numbers and emails.

Controls: least privilege for tools · keep instructions and data separate · human confirmation for irreversible actions · data provenance and validation · red teaming before launch (see the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications).
[0:16 · 3 min] Prompt injection has no complete fix today: design as if the model will sometimes follow injected instructions, and limit what it can do. Ask: "Your agent from S07 reads customer emails and can issue refunds. What could an attacker write in an email?"

Bias and fairness

Bias along the lifecycle

Bias enters at every step, not only in the data

1 · Data

Historical: the world as it was.

Representation: who is missing.

2 · Labels

Measurement: proxies that mean different things for different groups.

3 · Training

Aggregation: one model for all.

Learning: an objective that trades groups off.

4 · Evaluation

Evaluation: a benchmark that does not look like your users.

5 · Deployment

Deployment: used for something else, feedback loops.

For generative models: web-scale pretraining text, the pool of people who rate outputs during alignment, and the prompts themselves add new entry points. Framework: Suresh & Guttag (2021).
[0:24 · 3 min] Walk left to right with one example per step. Measurement bias: using "number of complaints" as a proxy for customer dissatisfaction penalises groups who complain less. Evaluation bias: testing a Spanish-language assistant only on Bogotá Spanish. Deployment bias: a model built to suggest becomes a model that decides.

Fairness metrics

Two common definitions of "fair"

Demographic parity

\( P(\hat Y = 1 \mid A = a) = P(\hat Y = 1 \mid A = b) \)

Same rate of favourable decisions in every group.

Credit: both groups are approved at the same rate.

Equal opportunity

\( P(\hat Y = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = a) = P(\hat Y = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = b) \)

Same true positive rate: people who deserve the favourable outcome get it equally often (Hardt, Price & Srebro, 2016).

Credit: among applicants who would repay, approval rates are equal.

You cannot have everything: when base rates differ between groups, a useful classifier cannot satisfy calibration and equal error rates at once (Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan, 2017; Chouldechova, 2017). Choose the metric from the harm you want to avoid, and document the choice.

[0:30 · 3 min] We report differences: DPD = rate(A) − rate(B) and EOD = TPR(A) − TPR(B); 0 is ideal. Equalized odds adds equal false positive rates. Reference text for everything in this part: Barocas, Hardt & Narayanan, "Fairness and Machine Learning" (free online book).

Counterfactual testing

Change only the person, keep everything else

Same sentence, one identity term swapped:

Carlos is a new customer of the bank.

María is a new customer of the bank.

A fair model gives (almost) the same output. We measure:

  • Mean score gap between group A and group B
  • Flip rate: share of pairs whose decision changes
  • DP and EO differences over the whole template set

Kiritchenko & Mohammad (2018) used templates like these to test 219 sentiment systems and found several with statistically significant gender or race bias. See also Garg et al. (2019).

Limits: templates are artificial; names are imperfect proxies for gender or origin; binary pairs leave people out; results in English do not transfer to Spanish.

[0:33 · 1 min] Short slide: the method behind the simulator and the lab. Emphasise the limits: a counterfactual audit is a smoke detector, not a certificate of fairness.

Check · fairness metrics

Andes Bank audits its loan model. Among applicants who actually repaid, group B was approved less often than group A. Which metric captures this problem?

Equal opportunity compares true positive rates: among people who deserve the favourable outcome (they repaid), each group should be approved equally often. Demographic parity ignores the true outcome; overall accuracy can look excellent while one group carries most of the errors.
[0:40 · 1 min] Quick poll, then click. Follow-up: "Which metric would a regulator care about if the two groups have different repayment rates?" There is no universal answer: that is the impossibility result.

Mitigation

Three places to intervene, plus the product

Before training

Rebalance or reweight data; counterfactual data augmentation: add copies with the attribute swapped (Zmigrod et al., 2019, studied it for languages with rich morphology such as Spanish).

During training

Fairness constraints or penalties in the loss; adversarial debiasing (Zhang, Lemoine & Mitchell, 2018).

After training

Group-specific thresholds (Hardt et al., 2016); output filters; averaging over counterfactual versions of the input.

Product controls: human review of adverse decisions, appeals, monitoring by group.

Group thresholds need the protected attribute at decision time (sensitive data, unequal treatment): get legal review and record the fairness–accuracy–cost trade-off.

[0:41 · 3 min] Business lens for Part 2. Every mitigation has a cost: accuracy, extra compute, or legal exposure. In the challenge students try two post-training mitigations and test whether they generalise to person terms that were not used to tune them. Spoiler: coverage is the weak point.

Privacy and regulation

[0:44] Part 3: about 18 minutes, including the breakout. Disclaimer: this is not legal advice; for a real deployment, involve your legal and data-protection team.

Privacy

Where personal data leaks into a generative system

  • Training and fine-tuning data: web scrapes, customer messages, tickets.
  • Prompts and files sent to external APIs; RAG knowledge bases; logs.
  • Memorisation: models can reproduce rare training strings verbatim (Carlini et al., 2021).
  • Re-identification: ZIP code + birth date + sex were enough to single out about 87% of the US population (Sweeney, 2000).

Truly anonymised data cannot be linked back to a person by any reasonably likely means, so it is no longer personal data (the anonymisation step itself is still processing). Pseudonymised data (names replaced by codes, identifiers removed but re-identification still possible) is still personal data (GDPR, Recital 26).

Toolbox: minimise what you collect · redact with regex + NER (lab) · keep pseudonym keys separate · aggregate · review samples by hand.

[0:44 · 3 min] The key distinction for the challenge's critique task: deleting names is not anonymisation. Quasi-identifiers (city, employer, dates, rare job titles) can re-identify people, especially when combined with other datasets.

Colombia

Ley 1581 de 2012: habeas data in practice

  • Applies to personal data: information linked to an identified or identifiable person. Regulated by Decreto 1377 de 2013.
  • Prior, express and informed authorisation for a stated purpose (principle of finality).
  • Sensitive data (health, biometrics, ethnic origin, political views, religion, sex life…) get reinforced protection.
  • People can know, update, rectify and request deletion of their data, or revoke authorisation.
  • International transfers only to countries with adequate protection, unless an exception applies.
  • Authority: the SIC. Its Circular Externa 002 de 2024 gives guidance on personal data in AI systems.

GDPR (EU) in comparison: similar principles, plus explicit data protection impact assessments (Art. 35) and rules on decisions based solely on automated processing (Art. 22).

NovaTel (fictional) wants to send support chats to a foreign LLM API. Before the pilot: purpose covered by the authorisation? Cross-border transfer allowed? Redaction in place? A contract that forbids the provider from reusing the data?

[0:47 · 3 min] Ask: "Whose authorisation did your company get for the data you plan to use in the project?" Link to the project rules: synthetic or anonymised data only. The SIC circular (August 2024) asks data controllers to apply suitability, necessity, reasonableness and proportionality before processing personal data with AI, and to abstain or take precautions when potential harms are uncertain.

EU AI Act · Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

Four risk tiers

Unacceptable → prohibitedSocial scoring · emotion recognition at work or school · untargeted face scraping · manipulation causing significant harm
High risk → strict obligationsAnnex III areas: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, access to essential services (e.g., credit scoring), law enforcement, migration, justice
Limited risk → transparencyTell people they talk to an AI; label deepfakes and AI-generated content
Minimal risk → no new obligationsSpam filters, games, most internal productivity uses

High-risk obligations: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, conformity assessment before market.

General-purpose AI models have their own duties (documentation, copyright policy, training-data summary), with more for models with systemic risk.

Not every face system is banned: prohibitions are specific (e.g., real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for policing, with narrow exceptions); other biometric systems are high-risk.

[0:51 · 2 min] The tier depends on the use, not on the technology: the same text classifier is minimal risk when it sorts reviews and high risk when it filters job applicants. Fines for prohibited practices can reach 35 million euros or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

Phased application: check the current status

  • 1 Aug 2024Entry into force

Does it apply to a Colombian company? Yes, if it places an AI system on the EU market or the system's output is used in the EU. Otherwise it is a useful reference, while Colombian law (e.g., Ley 1581) applies.

Sustainability

Where the emissions come from

$$ E\,[\text{kWh}] = P\,[\text{kW}] \times t\,[\text{h}] \times \text{PUE} \qquad \text{CO}_2\text{e}\,[\text{kg}] = E \times \text{grid intensity}\,[\text{kg/kWh}] $$

Training

Large, one-off, and repeated for every experiment. Strubell, Ganesh & McCallum (2019) made the energy cost of NLP visible and asked for reporting.

Inference

Small per request, huge at scale. Generative tasks use far more energy per request than classification, and multi-purpose models more than task-specific ones (Luccioni, Jernite & Strubell, 2024).

Beyond electricity

Manufacturing the hardware (embodied emissions), water for cooling, and the data centre's overhead (PUE = total energy ÷ IT energy).

[1:04 · 1 min] The same job can emit very different amounts depending on the hardware, the data centre and above all the grid. Colombia's grid is hydro-heavy, but its intensity rises in dry years when thermal plants run more; always use the official factor for the period (UPME publishes the national grid factor).

Check · carbon

A team fine-tunes a model for 100 GPU-hours. All else equal, which single change cuts emissions the most?

Emissions scale linearly with each factor: 0.15 / 0.8 is an 81% cut, PUE 1.1 / 1.2 about 8%, and 10% less time 10%. Doubling power for the same hours doubles energy. Often the biggest lever of all is not training a new model: a smaller or existing model may be enough.

Green AI

Levers you control

  • Right-size: small, distilled or task-specific models; LoRA instead of full fine-tuning (S04).
  • Fewer, smarter experiments: small subsets first, early stopping, efficient search (S11).
  • Efficient inference: batching, caching, quantisation (S11), shorter outputs.
  • Where and when: low-carbon regions, efficient data centres.
  • Measure and report: CodeCarbon or a cited method; report efficiency next to accuracy (Schwartz et al., 2020).

Energy is also cost. Every kWh saved is money saved. Put Wh per 1,000 requests next to accuracy and latency on the dashboard of NovaTel's assistant, and review it with the same seriousness.

Wrap-up

Key takeaways

  • Risks are specific: name them, give each an owner and a measurable control.
  • Bias can be measured: counterfactual pairs, DP and EO; the threshold is a fairness decision too.
  • Removing names is pseudonymisation, not anonymisation: Ley 1581 still applies.
  • The risk tier follows the use, not the technology; check current regulatory dates.
  • Energy = power × time × PUE; emissions depend on the grid. Measure, report and document it in a model card.
