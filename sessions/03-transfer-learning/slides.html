Warm-up · Q1

Which order is correct for one training step in PyTorch?

PyTorch accumulates gradients by default, so each step clears them, computes the loss, back-propagates and only then updates the weights. Forgetting zero_grad() was one of the planted bugs in the S02 challenge.
[0:02 · 3 min] Poll, then click. If people hesitate, remind them of the S02 "Crítica a la IA" task: missing zero_grad means gradients from previous batches keep adding up. Today the heavy lifting (pretraining) was done by someone else, but fine-tuning in S04 uses this exact loop.
Warm-up · Q2

Training loss keeps falling, but validation loss starts rising after epoch 6. What is the best reading?

A widening gap between training and validation loss is the signature of overfitting. Early stopping keeps the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss; dropout, weight decay and more data also help.
[0:05 · 2 min] Link to today: transfer learning is itself a defence against overfitting when labels are scarce, because most of the knowledge comes from pretraining instead of from your few labelled examples.
Warm-up · Q3

In a VAE, what usually happens when you increase β, the weight of the KL term?

The loss is reconstruction + β · KL. A larger β pushes the encoder's distribution towards \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\), which makes sampling from the prior work better at the cost of reconstruction detail.
[0:07 · 2 min] Ask two students who had different β values in the S02 challenge to compare their reconstruction and KL numbers in one sentence each.

[0:20] Part 2. Keep the maths light: one formula (attention) and one simulator.

The pipeline

From text to contextual vectors

1 · Tokens

A tokenizer splits text into pieces from a fixed vocabulary.

theref##undarrived

2 · Ids

Each token becomes an integer index.

19962541686303369

3 · Embeddings

Each id looks up a learned vector (768 numbers in BERT-base), plus a position vector.

4 · Transformer blocks

Attention mixes information between tokens, layer after layer (12 in BERT-base).

Output: one contextual vector per token. “bank” gets a different vector in “river bank” and in “bank loan”.

Positional information: attention on its own ignores word order, so the model adds position vectors: fixed sinusoids in the original Transformer, learned in BERT and GPT-2.
[0:20 · 3 min] The pieces and ids shown are the real bert-base-uncased output for "the refund arrived" (the lab prints them; [CLS] = 101 and [SEP] = 102 are added around them). Even a common English word like "refund" is split in two. The static embedding of "bank" is the same everywhere; after the attention layers the vector depends on the context. That is what "contextual embedding" means, and why these vectors are good features for classification.

Self-attention

Every token asks: who is relevant to me?

$$ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V $$

Query, key, value

Three learned projections of each token vector: \(Q = XW_Q\), \(K = XW_K\), \(V = XW_V\).

Scores → weights

\(QK^{\top}\) scores every pair of tokens; softmax turns each row into weights that sum to 1.

Why \(\sqrt{d_k}\)?

Dot products grow with the dimension \(d_k\). Dividing keeps softmax from saturating, which would leave tiny gradients.

The output for each token is a weighted average of the value vectors of all tokens it attends to (Vaswani et al., 2017).

[0:23 · 3 min] Read the formula right to left: compare my query with everyone's key, normalise with softmax, take the weighted average of their values. On the scaling: with components of variance 1, the dot product of two d_k-dimensional vectors has variance d_k; dividing by the square root brings it back to about 1 (Vaswani et al., section 3.2.1). The challenge's "Crítica a la IA" task touches this point.

That is the difference between an encoder (BERT, sees both sides) and a decoder (GPT, sees only the past). Real models have many heads per layer; tools such as BertViz show their actual weights.

Multi-head attention

Many heads, many layers

$$ \text{MultiHead}(X) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h)\, W^{O} $$
  • Each head has its own \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\), so it can specialise (coreference, syntax, position…).
  • BERT-base: 12 layers × 12 heads, hidden size 768, so \(d_k = 768 / 12 = 64\).
  • Each block: attention → add & normalise → feed-forward network → add & normalise.

Encoder vs decoder

Encoder: every token attends to every token (bidirectional). Good for understanding: classification, extraction, embeddings.

Decoder: a causal mask hides future tokens. Good for generating text one token at a time.

Encoder–decoder: the decoder also attends to the encoder's outputs (cross-attention).

[0:31 · 2 min] Heads run in parallel on smaller projections and their outputs are concatenated. The residual connections ("add") and layer normalisation make deep stacks trainable. The encoder/decoder distinction is exactly the mask you toggled in the simulator.

WordPiece and BPE: sub-words, not words

WordPiece · BERT

Vocabulary of 30,522 pieces; ## marks a continuation. bert-base-uncased also lowercases and strips accents.

Vocabulary of 50,257 pieces built by merging frequent byte pairs; Ġ marks a leading space, and accented letters cost extra bytes.

Frequent words in the training corpus get one token; rare words, and most Spanish words for English-centric tokenizers, get split into several. BPE: Sennrich, Haddow & Birch (2016).

In-context learning

Zero-shot and few-shot prompting


messages = [
  {"role": "system", "content": "Classify the news article. "
   "Answer with one label: World, Sports, Business, Sci/Tech."},
  # few-shot: solved examples as previous turns
  {"role": "user", "content": "Article: Stocks rallied after…"},
  {"role": "assistant", "content": "Business"},
  {"role": "user", "content": "Article: " + new_article},
]
inputs = tok.apply_chat_template(messages, add_generation_prompt=True,
                                 return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)
  • No weight updates: the examples live only in this prompt (Brown et al., 2020).
  • Output is free text: parse it into a label and count failures as unknown.
  • Results depend on the choice, order and format of the examples (Zhao et al., 2021).
  • Every example is paid for in tokens and latency, on every request.
[0:49 · 2 min] This is exactly the lab's code with Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct. Emphasise the first bullet: the model does not "remember" the examples after the call; the challenge's AI-critique task plants this misconception. Zhao et al. (2021) show how sensitive few-shot accuracy is to example order and to a bias towards labels that appear often in the prompt.

Feature extraction

Frozen encoder + a small head


@torch.no_grad()
def embed(texts):
    enc = tok(texts, padding=True, truncation=True,
              return_tensors="pt")
    h = encoder(**enc).last_hidden_state    # (batch, tokens, 384)
    mask = enc["attention_mask"].unsqueeze(-1)
    e = (h * mask).sum(1) / mask.sum(1)     # mean pooling
    return F.normalize(e, dim=1).numpy()

clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(embed(X_train), y_train)
  • all-MiniLM-L6-v2: a 6-layer encoder tuned for sentence similarity (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019): 384 numbers per text.
  • Compute embeddings once; the head trains in seconds on a CPU.
  • Predictions are fast and deterministic, and the head is easy to audit.
  • The encoder never learns your task: accuracy has a ceiling.
[0:51 · 2 min] Mean pooling averages the token vectors, ignoring padding thanks to the attention mask; this is what the sentence-transformers library does for this model, and the lab checks that both give the same vectors. The head can be any classic model: logistic regression, an SVM or a small MLP (S08).

Changing the weights

Fine-tuning and domain adaptation

Partial fine-tuning

Unfreeze the top layers and the head. Cheaper, less risk of damaging general knowledge. Gradual unfreezing: Howard & Ruder (2018).

Full fine-tuning

Update every weight with a small learning rate. Usually the best accuracy with enough labels; risk of catastrophic forgetting. Session 04.

Domain adaptation

Continue masked-LM pretraining on unlabelled in-domain text (clinical notes, contracts), then fine-tune. Gururangan et al. (2020) report consistent gains from this second pretraining phase.

Same data, same split: compare strategies on the same held-out test set, or the comparison means nothing.
[0:53 · 2 min] Fine-tuning is next session's topic, so stay conceptual. Domain-adaptive pretraining ("Don't stop pretraining") is the answer when your texts use a language the pretrained model rarely saw: medical abbreviations, legal Spanish, internal jargon. The warning is the lab's comparison rule.

Wrap-up

Key takeaways

  • Self-supervised pretraining turns unlabelled text into reusable knowledge, biases included.
  • Attention lets every token weigh every other token; the causal mask makes a decoder.
  • Tokens are the unit of cost: measure your tokenizer on Spanish text.
  • Climb the transfer spectrum only as far as your data, budget and latency justify.
