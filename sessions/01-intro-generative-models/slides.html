Warm-up · Q1

When a chat assistant writes an answer, what is the underlying model doing at each step?

A language model writes one token at a time: it scores every possible next token, turns the scores into probabilities and picks one. Products may add search or tools on top (RAG, agents), but the core step is next-token prediction.
[0:05 · 2 min] Poll first, then click. Common misconception: "it searches the internet". Some products do call a search tool, but the model itself only predicts tokens. We will see the probability distribution live in about 15 minutes.
Warm-up · Q2

You ask the same question twice and get two different answers. What is the most likely reason?

With sampling (temperature above 0) the model can pick a different token each time, and one different word changes everything after it. Chatting does not change the weights: training is a separate, offline process.
[0:07 · 2 min] This answer is the seed of today's challenge: students will measure how much outputs vary with temperature. Clarify that providers may use conversations to train future versions (depending on their terms), but not in real time during your chat.

Part 1

What is generative AI?

From predicting labels to producing new data.

Theory · 60'
[0:10] Theory block starts: four parts of about 15 minutes each, with an interaction every 8 to 10 minutes.

Two kinds of models

Discriminative vs generative

🏷️

Discriminative

Learns \(p(y \mid x)\): the label given the input.

“Is this email spam?” · credit scoring · churn prediction · sentiment

Output: a label or a score.

✨

Generative

Learns \(p(x)\) or \(p(x, y)\): how the data itself is distributed.

Sample new data \(x \sim p(x)\): a new email, image, melody or function.

Output: new content.

Bayes' rule links them: \(p(y \mid x) = p(x \mid y)\,p(y) / p(x)\). A generative model can also classify (Session 08); a discriminative model cannot generate.
[0:10 · 3 min] Keep it intuitive. Discriminative: draw a boundary between classes. Generative: learn what the data looks like, well enough to produce new examples. Business translation: most "classic" ML in companies (scoring, churn) is discriminative; generative AI produces content. Ask: "Is a fraud detector discriminative or generative?" (discriminative).

One idea, many modalities

Learn the distribution, then sample from it

📝 Text

Chat assistants, summaries, translation, drafting emails.

🖼️ Images

Text-to-image generation, editing, product mock-ups (e.g., Stable Diffusion; Rombach et al., 2022).

🔊 Audio

Synthetic voices (text-to-speech), music, sound effects.

💻 Code

Code completion and generation from natural language (e.g., Codex; Chen et al., 2021).

Usually the model is conditioned on an input: \(p(x \mid \text{prompt})\).

[0:13 · 2 min] Ask for one example per modality from their own work (chat). Point out that the underlying recipe is the same: a model of the data distribution plus a sampling procedure. Conditioning on a prompt is what turns a generator into a useful tool.

Inside a language model

Writing one token at a time

Text is split into tokens (words or word pieces). An autoregressive model factorises the probability of a text:

$$ p(x_1, \dots, x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid x_{<t}) $$

prompt → model → logits → softmax(T) → top-k → sample → next token ↺

Softmax with temperature

\( p_i = \dfrac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)} \)

Top-k

Keep only the \(k\) most likely tokens, renormalise, then sample. \(T \to 0\) or \(k = 1\) is greedy decoding.

[0:15 · 3 min] Walk the loop: the model outputs one score (logit) per vocabulary token; softmax turns scores into probabilities; temperature divides the logits first (low T sharpens, high T flattens); top-k cuts the long tail; we sample; the chosen token is appended and the loop repeats. This is exactly what students will code with generate() in the lab.

Customer-facing risk

Fluent is not the same as correct

NovaTel (a fictional telecom) lets a model draft replies to billing complaints. The model optimises plausibility, not truth: it can invent a refund policy that sounds exactly right.

What a manager should ask

In today's lab you will use an autoencoding classifier, BERT's masked-word predictor and an autoregressive chat model.

[0:28 · 3 min] The Transformer is the shared building block; what differs is which tokens each position can attend to and what the model is trained to predict. Encoders see the whole sentence and are great at understanding; decoders see only the past and are natural generators; encoder-decoders read a whole input and write a new output.

Beyond text

VAEs, GANs and diffusion models

VAE

An encoder compresses \(x\) into a latent code \(z\); a decoder rebuilds \(x\). Trained with reconstruction loss plus a KL term. Sample a new \(z\) → new data.

Kingma & Welling (2014) · you train one in S02

GAN

A generator forges samples; a discriminator tries to tell real from fake. They improve by competing. Sharp images, but training can be unstable.

Goodfellow et al. (2014)

Diffusion

Learns to reverse a gradual noising process: generation starts from pure noise and removes it step by step. Used in many text-to-image systems.

Ho, Jain & Abbeel (2020)

Reference text: Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville (2016), Deep Learning, ch. 20 covers deep generative models in depth.

Foundation models

Pretrain once, adapt many times

Broad data

Web text, books, code; mostly unlabelled.

→

Pretraining

Self-supervised, at scale: next token or masked token.

→

Adaptation

Prompting, RAG, fine-tuning → many downstream tasks.

A foundation model is trained on broad data, usually with self-supervision at scale, and can be adapted to a wide range of tasks (Bommasani et al., 2021).

Emergence: capabilities appear that nobody trained for explicitly, such as following instructions given only in the prompt.
Homogenization: many applications share the same few models, so they also share their defects.
[0:39 · 3 min] The term comes from the Stanford report by Bommasani and colleagues (2021). Self-supervision means the labels come from the data itself: hide a word and predict it. That is why pretraining can use enormous unlabelled corpora. Emergence and homogenization are the two concepts the report highlights; homogenization sets up the business lens.

Size matters, differently

The models you will actually run

≈1,300×

fewer parameters in SmolLM2-135M (today's lab) than in GPT-3, which has 175 billion (Brown et al., 2020). Arithmetic: 175,000 M ÷ 135 M ≈ 1,296.

  • Small open models run on a free Kaggle GPU, sometimes on a laptop CPU.
  • They are weaker, but cheap, fast, private and fully under your control.
  • Parameter count is not quality: it drives cost, latency and where the model can run.

SmolLM2: Allal et al. (2025).

[0:42 · 1 min] Quick slide. We deliberately use small open models all semester so that everything runs on free compute and students can fine-tune them. The trade-off (capability vs cost and control) is the heart of Part 3.

Vendor dependence

Homogenization is a supplier risk

Andes Bank (fictional) builds its chatbot, its email summaries and its fraud-alert explanations on the same foundation model from one provider.

One provider update, or one deprecated model version, changes all three products at once.

Questions to ask any model provider

  • How are model versions pinned, updated and retired?
  • Is our data stored, used for training, or processed abroad?
  • Can we test a new version on our own cases before switching?

Your own evaluation set is your insurance (Sessions 05 and 11).

[0:43 · 2 min] Business lens for Part 2. Shared foundation models concentrate risk: a single change propagates everywhere. The practical defence is a small, representative evaluation set that you run before any model change. That idea returns in S05 and S11.

Part 3

Own models vs APIs

Where on the adaptation spectrum should your use case sit?

[0:45] Part 3: the business core of the session and of the challenge's "Explica y decide" task.

The adaptation spectrum

Four ways to make a model yours

1 · Prompting

Change: instructions and examples in the prompt.

Needs: a good prompt and test cases. Hours to days.

2 · RAG

Change: the context: retrieve your documents and add them to the prompt.

Needs: clean, searchable documents. Days to weeks.

3 · Fine-tuning

Change: the weights, starting from a pretrained model (full or LoRA).

Needs: curated examples and a GPU. Weeks.

4 · From scratch

Change: everything; the weights start random.

Needs: very large data, compute and a specialised team. Months.

← faster · cheaper · less datamore control · specialisation · cost →

They combine: a fine-tuned model can also use RAG. RAG: Lewis et al. (2020) · LoRA: Hu et al. (2022).

[0:45 · 3 min] The time ranges are qualitative orders of magnitude, not benchmarks. Prompting changes nothing in the model; RAG changes what the model reads; fine-tuning changes the weights; training from scratch builds new weights. Only the last two produce a model that is "yours" in the sense of the final project. Point out that most real systems combine steps.

Build vs buy

Four lenses: cost, control, privacy, IP

Buy: vendor APIBuild: own or open-weights model
CostPay per token; no infrastructure; the bill grows with volumeUpfront work, GPUs or servers, operations; low cost per extra request
ControlThe vendor decides updates, limits and retirementsYou pin versions, decide when to update and can change the weights
PrivacyData leaves your perimeter; you rely on contract termsData can stay on your own infrastructure
IP & licencesRead the provider's terms on data and outputsRead the model licence: open weights ≠ unrestricted use; your fine-tuned weights are an asset

The options are not exclusive: you can call an API for one task and run your own model for another.

Total cost of ownership

The token bill is only one line

50 M

tokens per month for 50,000 conversations of about 1,000 tokens each (illustrative arithmetic). Multiply by your provider's current price per million tokens, then add the rest.

Beyond tokens, budget for:

  • Data preparation and labelling
  • Evaluation before launch and after every change
  • Monitoring, incidents and human review
  • People, training and change management
  • Switching costs if the provider changes terms

Lab preview

Two pretrained models in a few lines


from transformers import pipeline, AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM

clf = pipeline("sentiment-analysis",
               model="distilbert/distilbert-base-uncased-finetuned-sst-2-english")
print(clf("The new app is fast and easy to use"))   # [{'label': 'POSITIVE', ...}]

name = "HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-135M-Instruct"
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(name)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(name)
messages = [{"role": "user", "content": "Write a slogan for a coffee shop."}]
inputs = tok.apply_chat_template(messages, add_generation_prompt=True,
                                 return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)
out = model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=40, do_sample=True, temperature=0.7)
print(tok.decode(out[0, inputs["input_ids"].shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True))

Wrap-up

Key takeaways

  • Generative models learn \(p(x)\) and sample from it; language models do it one token at a time.
  • Temperature and top-k trade consistency for diversity; neither guarantees truth.
  • Pick the model family by the task: encoders understand, decoders generate.
  • Prompting → RAG → fine-tuning → from scratch: more control, more cost.
